about

  

    
BML is a small implementation of Standard ML, the lexer, the parser, the
    type inference, the standard library, all of it short enough that one person
    can hold it in mind and follow what happens to a program between the typing
    and the answer.


    
The compiler is opaque, the type error arrives from nowhere, and the
    student learns to propitiate the machine rather than to understand it.
    Iteracy, by which I mean the capacity to read and write computational
    processes rather than merely operate them, depends on there being something
    available to read, and an instrument whose parameters are visible is a
    different sort of object from one whose parameters are hidden. A teaching
    interpreter can be the first sort, and this one tries to be. The standard
    library is written in BML itself and loaded as source, so the
    map you call is the map you could have written, and
    you can open the file and confirm that it is.


    
The whole language is running in this page. There is no server behind the
    prompt, nothing is sent anywhere, and the files answering here are the files
    in the repository.

BML vs SML

  

    
    
BML is being developed to capture all of SML functionality. Most of
    Harper's course examples run as written. What is missing:

    

      
the BasisThe thinnest part, and being filled in.
          Nineteen structures. No IntInf, Word8 or the
          monomorphic arrays.
          No OS, and there will not be one, since nothing behind
          this has a file system. Date is UTC only: local time
          needs a timezone and there is no operating system here to hold
          one.

      
operatorsAn operator cannot be DEFINED in its infix form,
          fun (f ** g) (x, y) = …, and op cannot stand
          in a pattern.

      
blockslet … end works as an operator's left
          operand but not its right, and open cannot appear inside
          a let.

      
the stackNon-tail recursion is bounded by the host stack.
          Tail calls are proper, as the Definition requires.

    

  




  
POP-11 and Poplog

  

    
POP-11 was written at Sussex in 1975, became the core of
    Poplog, ran in UK departments through the 1980s and 1990s, and acquired a
    Standard ML in the end.

where it started

    
POP-2 was an Edinburgh language. Aaron Sloman met it in early 1972 over a
    link to Edinburgh's Elliott 4130, and spent 1972 to 1973 in the Department of
    Computational Logic there, learning AI under Bernard Meltzer.


    
Sussex, October 1973

    
Sloman returned and, with Max Clowes and colleagues, set up the Cognitive
    Studies Programme. The question in front of them was which language could
    serve beginners and research at the same time. They weighed LOGO, Lisp,
    Prolog and Algol-68, and chose POP-2 as the base to build on.


    
the implementation

    
Steve Hardy arrived in October 1974 and wrote the first POP-11 over the
    summer of 1975, on a PDP-11/40, finishing in January 1976. He called it
    POP-11 because Julian Davies had called his DEC-10 system POP-10. The move to
    VAX in 1981 was the making of it: John Gibson reimplemented POP-11 for VMS,
    procedures compiled all the way to machine code rather than being
    interpreted, and the bootstrap ran through earlier versions written in
    assembler and C.


    
what was distinctive

    
A pattern matcher with segment variables, so
    [== [== ^item1 ??found ^item2 ==] ==] did work that took a page
    of Lisp or Pascal. Numerical indexing, item(5), which grew into
    a facility for treating any object as a procedure. An incremental compiler.
    dlocal, a generalisation of Lisp's unwind-protect that restored
    values on any exit including an abnormal one. And multi-branch conditionals
    written if … then … elseif … then … else … endif rather than as
    nested parentheses.


    
why it spread

    
POP-11 ran at Nottingham, Aberdeen, Warwick, Queen Mary College and
    Sheffield as well as Sussex. A survey of Alvey-funded UK projects found three
    languages rated good by all their users, POP-11, PARLOG and C++, and of those
    POP-11 had the most. Sloman put the reach down to syntax, arguing that the AI
    languages had not been taken up by programmers outside AI simply because of
    the syntax of Lisp. POP-11 offered the same power in a shape a Pascal
    programmer could read.
